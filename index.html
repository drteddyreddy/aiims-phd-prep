INFO · PSY02

Your Project: CBD × Alcohol Detox

Everything you need to discuss PSY02 like it's yours — the clinical problem, why CBD, the honest evidence, the trial design, and the Indian rules — plus panel-ready one-liners. This feeds both your subject MCQs and the interview.

🎯 The project in one sentence

PSY02 — "Cannabidiol as an adjunctive short-term treatment during inpatient detoxification in patients with alcohol dependence: a multicenter Phase III RCT." You are testing whether adding CBD to standard benzodiazepine detox makes withdrawal safer and easier — chiefly by being benzodiazepine-sparing and anti-craving — in a rigorous trial that fills a real evidence gap.

💡 The framing to hold in your head

CBD is an adjunct, not a replacement. Benzodiazepines stay the backbone of detox; CBD is added on top to target what benzos don't — craving, neurotoxicity, liver stress — and to let you use less benzodiazepine. And the honest position on evidence: preclinical strong, human data early, no Phase III yet — which is exactly why this trial exists. Never overstate it.

1 · The clinical problem

Alcohol dependence is common and its management starts with detoxification — the medically supervised, short-term treatment of the withdrawal syndrome. In India it is a large, under-treated burden: NMHS 2015–16 put AUD at ~4.6–4.7% with a treatment gap near 86%, and the Magnitude of Substance Use 2019 survey found alcohol the commonest psychoactive substance (14.6%, ~16 crore users).

🧠 Why withdrawal is dangerous — the neurobiology

Alcohol potentiates GABA-A and inhibits NMDA/glutamate. Chronic use adapts by down-regulating GABA and up-regulating NMDA/glutamate. Stop drinking and you get unopposed glutamatergic excitation → tremor, seizures, and delirium tremens. Repeated unmedicated withdrawals kindle — each one gets worse — which is the whole argument for treating detox properly rather than "toughing it out."

Benzodiazepines — the standard of care

Benzodiazepines are the gold standard for alcohol detox. They are cross-tolerant with alcohol (both enhance GABA-A), so they replace alcohol's missing GABA drive and prevent seizures and DTs. Long-acting agents (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam) give a smoother self-taper; lorazepam/oxazepam are preferred in liver disease, the elderly, and respiratory compromise (glucuronidated, no active metabolites — mnemonic LOT). Dosing is usually symptom-triggered by CIWA-Ar, which uses less total drug than fixed schedules.

The unmet needs — where an adjunct could help

Benzodiazepine load

Benzos sedate, carry their own dependence and abuse liability, risk over-sedation/respiratory depression, and complicate the hepatically-impaired. Less benzo, same safety, is a genuine clinical win.

Craving

Benzos do little for craving. Craving during and just after detox drives early relapse — the point where most detox "successes" fail.

Neurotoxicity & liver

Alcohol is directly neurotoxic and hepatotoxic. Standard detox controls symptoms but doesn't actively protect the brain or liver.

2 · Why CBD as an adjunct

CBD is a non-psychoactive phytocannabinoid with "promiscuous" (multi-target) pharmacology. It has very low affinity at CB1 (acts as a negative allosteric modulator), so it does not produce a high, and it hits a spread of other targets that each map onto a detox need.

CBD mechanismPharmacology→ Detox benefit
Anxiolytic 5-HT1A agonism (also ↑ anandamide tone) Eases withdrawal-related anxiety / dysphoria; may reduce the benzodiazepine needed to settle a patient
Anti-craving 5-HT1A + indirect endocannabinoid boost (FAAH inhibition → ↑ anandamide) Reduces cue- and stress-induced reinstatement in animal models → lowers relapse pressure during and after detox
Neuroprotective Antioxidant + 5-HT1A-mediated; anti-excitotoxic Mitigates alcohol-induced neurodegeneration (binge models) — protects against the excitotoxic storm of withdrawal
Hepatoprotective / anti-inflammatory PPARγ agonism; anti-oxidative Attenuates alcoholic steatosis and oxidative stress in rodents — attractive in a population with frequent liver injury
Favourable safety Non-psychoactive (CB1 low-affinity/NAM); low abuse liability (WHO 2018) An adjunct that adds no intoxication and no abuse potential — safe to layer onto benzodiazepine detox
🧩 Remember CBD's targets — "5-TGPF"

5-HT1A agonist · TRPV1 agonist (desensitizes) · GPR55 antagonist · PPARγ agonist · FAAH inhibitor (↑ anandamide) — plus CB1 negative allosteric modulator. The 5-HT1A action is the one to name first for anxiolytic/anti-craving effects.

⚠ Be honest about CBD's own risks (a strength in interview, not a weakness)

CBD is not risk-free. It causes dose-related transaminase (ALT/AST) elevations, so LFT monitoring is built into the protocol and significant hepatic impairment is an exclusion/caution. It is a broad CYP inhibitor (CYP3A4, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP1A2) with drug-interaction potential — the classic exam fact is the CBD–clobazam interaction (↑ N-desmethylclobazam → sedation). Naming these unprompted shows you understand your own molecule.

3 · The honest evidence state

This is the section the panel will probe hardest. Get the calibration right: strong signal in animals, thin but promising in humans, and no definitive trial yet.

Preclinical — strong

Consistent rodent evidence for anti-craving (reduced cue/stress reinstatement), neuroprotection and hepatoprotection. This is the foundation.

Human — early & small

A single 800 mg oral dose reduced alcohol craving and reward-region fMRI activity (Heidelberg). A 2025 preliminary RCT of hemp-CBD in AUD showed safety/tolerability in small samples; a youth-AUD RCT examined neural effects. Signals, not proof.

Phase III — the gap

No large Phase III RCT has established efficacy for withdrawal severity or relapse prevention. That gap is precisely what PSY02 is built to fill.

⚠ Do NOT say this

Never claim CBD is "proven" to treat AUD, "cures" craving, or "replaces" benzodiazepines. The correct posture is: "The preclinical rationale is strong and early human signals are encouraging, but efficacy in detox is unproven — which is the justification for a rigorous Phase III trial."

4 · The trial design

A multicenter Phase III RCT of CBD as an adjuvant — CBD (or matched placebo) added on top of standard benzodiazepine-based detox. Know every design word below; each defends against a specific bias.

Design featureWhat it meansWhy it's there
RandomisedPatients allocated to CBD vs placebo by chanceBalances known and unknown confounders across arms
Double-blindNeither patient nor assessor knows the armRemoves performance & detection bias (CIWA-Ar is rater-scored)
Placebo-controlledComparator is matched placebo plus standard benzo careIsolates CBD's added effect; ethical because everyone still gets standard detox
Parallel-groupEach patient stays in one armClean comparison; no carry-over (crossover unsuitable for acute detox)
MulticenterSeveral sitesGeneralizability + faster recruitment to hit the sample size
Allocation concealmentThe upcoming assignment is hidden from enrollersStops selection bias at the point of enrolment (distinct from blinding)
Stratified / block randomisationRandomise within strata (site, baseline severity)Keeps arms balanced on strong prognostic factors

Population

DSM-5 alcohol dependence admitted for inpatient detox, with a-priori inclusion/exclusion. Significant hepatic impairment is a caution/exclusion given CBD's transaminase effect — a design point that shows you've thought about safety.

Endpoints

Primary (choose one, pre-specified)

  • Withdrawal severity — CIWA-Ar (peak or area-under-curve over the detox), or
  • Benzodiazepine-sparing — total diazepam-equivalent dose across the detox episode

Benzo-sparing is the cleaner "so-what" endpoint — a concrete, clinically meaningful reduction in drug load.

Secondary

  • Craving — OCDS / VAS
  • Anxiety & depression scores; sleep
  • Treatment retention / completion
  • Relapse at follow-up
  • LFTs (safety + hepatoprotection)
  • Adverse events

Governance, ethics & statistics

Governance & ethics
  • DSMB — independent safety monitoring, interim analyses, stopping rules
  • ICH-GCP compliance; SAE reporting
  • Institutional ethics committee approval + informed consent
  • CTRI registration (mandatory in India) — prospective, transparent
Statistics
  • A-priori sample size / power from a target effect on the primary endpoint
  • ITT analysis — analyse as randomised (preserves randomisation, avoids attrition bias)
  • Pre-registered protocol — endpoints fixed before data
  • Pre-specified handling of missing data

5 · Indian regulatory context

You will be asked how a "cannabis" trial is even legal in India. The answer hinges on the NDPS definition and on CBD's regulatory status.

PointDetail
NDPS Act, 1985 — what "cannabis" is Defines cannabis as charas (resin), ganja (flowering/fruiting tops), and any mixture/drink of these. Seeds and leaves (without the tops) are excluded — so bhang (leaf preparation) sits outside the central NDPS definition and is state-regulated.
Where CBD sits Pure CBD is not separately scheduled under NDPS. Hemp-derived CBD with <0.3% THC is treated leniently; THC is the controlled molecule. Marijuana-derived / high-THC CBD is prohibited.
CBD as a drug Isolated CBD is not an approved allopathic drug in India. Using it as an investigational medicinal product therefore needs the formal research route.
Research route CDSCO approval (new-drug / clinical-trial permission) + institutional ethics clearance + CTRI registration before enrolment. FSSAI (2021) separately recognised hemp seed/products (<0.3% THC) as food — not the pathway for a drug trial.
💡 One-liner on legality

"We use a purified, low-THC CBD investigational product. THC — not CBD — is what NDPS controls, and pure CBD isn't separately scheduled. As an unapproved drug it goes through the research pathway: CDSCO permission, ethics approval, and CTRI registration."

6 · If the panel asks… → say this

❓ "Why CBD — why not just give more benzodiazepines?"

Say: "Benzodiazepines are and stay the backbone — they prevent seizures and DTs. But more benzo means more sedation, more dependence/abuse liability, and more risk in the hepatically-impaired, and it does little for craving or neurotoxicity. CBD is a non-sedating, non-addictive adjunct that targets exactly those gaps, with the goal of being benzodiazepine-sparing — not benzodiazepine-replacing."

❓ "What is your primary endpoint?"

Say: "Either withdrawal severity on CIWA-Ar (peak/AUC) or, my preference, benzodiazepine-sparing — total diazepam-equivalent dose over the detox episode, because it's concrete and clinically meaningful. Craving (OCDS), retention, relapse, LFTs and AEs are secondary. It's pre-specified with an a-priori sample size and ITT analysis."

❓ "Isn't the evidence for CBD weak?"

Say: "The preclinical evidence is genuinely strong, and early human signals — an 800 mg dose reducing craving and reward-region activity, small pilot RCTs showing safety — are encouraging. But no Phase III has established efficacy in detox. That's not a reason to dismiss CBD; it's the justification for doing the definitive trial. A rigorous RCT is how you generate that evidence rather than assume it."

❓ "Is it safe? What about CBD's liver toxicity and interactions?"

Say: "CBD is non-psychoactive with low abuse liability (WHO 2018). The real safety flags are dose-related transaminase elevations and its CYP-inhibition interactions — the classic one being CBD–clobazam. We manage that by excluding significant hepatic impairment, monitoring LFTs on schedule, screening for interacting drugs, and independent DSMB oversight. Studying it in a monitored inpatient setting is the safest place to do it."

🎤 30-second interview pitch (memorise)

"Alcohol detox is benzodiazepine-based and effective at preventing seizures, but it doesn't address craving, neurotoxicity or liver injury, and carries its own sedation and dependence load. CBD — non-psychoactive, 5-HT1A anxiolytic and anti-craving, neuro- and hepatoprotective, low abuse liability — is a plausible adjunct. Preclinical evidence is strong, human data early, and no Phase III has proven efficacy. My project is a multicenter, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase III RCT testing whether adjunctive CBD reduces withdrawal severity and is benzodiazepine-sparing, with craving, retention, relapse and LFTs as secondary outcomes — done to full GCP with DSMB oversight and CTRI registration."

5 · The NDPS Act, 1985 — law & policy

Controls Narcotic Drugs & Psychotropic Substances (amended 1989, 2001, 2014). Penalties are graded by quantity — small vs commercial — with intermediate quantities in between. NCB (Narcotics Control Bureau) is the nodal enforcement agency.

SectionProvision
Sec 27Punishment for consumption of narcotic drugs / psychotropic substances
Sec 64AImmunity from prosecution for addicts who volunteer for de-addiction treatment (the "treatment not jail" provision)
Sec 39Court may release an addict for treatment in lieu of / alongside sentence (court-ordered treatment)
⚠ Alcohol is NOT under NDPS

NDPS is a Central Act; alcohol is a State subject (Excise). So alcohol policy — including prohibition — varies by state: dry states include Gujarat, Bihar, Nagaland, Mizoram, and Lakshadweep. Cannabis definition under NDPS excludes seeds/leaves, so bhang is state-regulated too.

Central tendency & spread

  • Mean — symmetric/normal data; dragged by outliers. Median — skewed data / outliers (robust). Mode — only measure for nominal.
  • Skew rule: right (positive) skew → mean > median > mode; the mean "chases the tail."
  • Spread: Range < IQR (robust, pairs with median) · Variance (σ²) · SD (√variance, same units) · CV = SD/mean × 100% (unitless — compares across scales).
🔑 Normal distribution — 68–95–99.7

Symmetric; mean = median = mode. ±1 SD ≈ 68%, ±2 SD ≈ 95%, ±3 SD ≈ 99.7%. Exactly 95% lies within ±1.96 SD.

SD vs SEM, and the CLT

✓ Don't confuse SD and SEM

SD = spread of individual observations. SEM = SD/√n = precision of the sample mean → shrinks as n grows. CLT: the sampling distribution of the mean approaches normal as n rises (n≥30), whatever the population shape.

Hypothesis testing

TermMeaning
Type I error (α)Reject a true H₀ = false positive (usually 0.05)
Type II error (β)Fail to reject a false H₀ = false negative

⚠ Statistical ≠ clinical significance

A huge sample can make a trivial difference "p<0.05." Significance ≠ importance. We reject or fail to reject H₀ — never "accept" it.

Confidence intervals & effect measures

  • 95% CI for a difference that excludes 0 → p<0.05. For RR/OR, one that excludes 1 → significant.
  • RR (cohort/RCT) = incidence exposed / unexposed. OR (case-control). OR ≈ RR when disease is rare.
  • HR = time-to-event (Cox/Kaplan–Meier). RR/OR/HR = 1 → no association. NNT = 1/ARR.

🧠 Incidence vs prevalence

2 · Research Methodology

🔑 Evidence hierarchy (weak → strong)

Case report/series → cross-sectional (prevalence, snapshot) → case-control (retrospective, starts with outcome, rare diseases, gives OR, recall bias) → cohort (starts with exposure, gives RR/incidence, rare exposures) → RCT (causation) → systematic review / meta-analysis (apex).

RCT concepts

  • Randomization → balances known and unknown confounders
  • Allocation concealment → hides the next assignment (prevents selection bias) — ≠ blinding
  • Blinding → reduces performance/observer bias
  • ITT (analyse as randomized, conservative) vs per-protocol (completers, exaggerates efficacy)

Trial phases

  • I — safety/dose, 20–100 (often healthy)
  • II — efficacy + safety, 100–300 patients
  • III — vs standard, up to ~3000, pivotal for approval (= your CBD study)
  • IV — post-marketing surveillance, rare/long-term effects
⚠ Bias you must recognise on sight

Recall bias → case-control (cases remember exposures better). Lead-time / length-time bias → screening. Confounder = linked to exposure AND independently causes outcome (control via randomization, matching, stratification, regression). Effect modification = a real subgroup difference to report, not a bias to remove.

✓ Ethics & reporting (India-flavoured)

Belmont (1979): Respect for persons · Beneficence · Justice. Helsinki (WMA 1964), Nuremberg (1947, consent). ICMR National Ethical Guidelines 2017 = India's document; trials also need CDSCO/Schedule Y + CTRI registration. Reporting: CONSORT=RCT · STROBE=observational · PRISMA=reviews · STARD=diagnostic.

DSM-5 AUD vs ICD-11 dependence

DSM-5 — Alcohol Use Disorder

  • Single spectrum: abuse + dependence merged.
  • ≥2 of 11 criteria within 12 months.
  • Severity: mild 2–3 · moderate 4–5 · severe ≥6.
  • Craving added as a criterion; legal-problems criterion dropped.

ICD-11 — Alcohol dependence

  • ≥2 of 3 core clusters over ≥12 mo (or continuous ≥1 mo):
  • ① Impaired control over use
  • ② Increasing salience / priority over other activities
  • ③ Physiological features — tolerance, withdrawal, or use to relieve withdrawal
🔑 ICD-10 → ICD-11 changes

ICD-10 needed 3 of 6 features; ICD-11 streamlines to 3 clusters, 2 required. ICD-11 adds a "harmful pattern of use" category and a single-episode of harmful use, and now codes intoxication, withdrawal and dependence per substance.

⚠ Tolerance vs Withdrawal (don't swap them)

Tolerance = need for an increased dose for the same effect (pharmacodynamic + metabolic adaptation). Withdrawal = a substance-specific syndrome on cessation/reduction, relieved by re-dosing. Both fall inside the physiological cluster.

✓ Hallucinosis vs DTs — the classic MCQ

Alcoholic hallucinosis = hallucinations with a clear, oriented sensorium and near-normal vitals (12–24 h). DTs = clouded/fluctuating consciousness + autonomic hyperactivity + disorientation (48–72 h). The distinguishing feature asked is the sensorium, not the presence of tremor.

CIWA-Ar severity (10 items, max 67)

  • <8–10 — minimal
  • 8–15 — mild
  • 15–20 — moderate
  • >20 — severe

Drives symptom-triggered dosing — usually medicate at ≥8–10.

Risk factors for severe withdrawal / DTs

  • Prior DTs or withdrawal seizures (kindling)
  • High / prolonged intake; prior detox history
  • Older age; concurrent illness / infection
  • ↑ baseline heart rate
  • Low K⁺ / Mg²⁺, thrombocytopenia
⚠ DTs mortality

Roughly 1–5% treated, historically up to ~15–37% untreated. Hallmark = fluctuating consciousness + autonomic instability. A true medical emergency, not "just" withdrawal.

3 · Detoxification Pharmacology

Benzodiazepines — gold standard

  • BZDs are first-line — cross-tolerant with alcohol (both ↑ GABA-A), reduce seizures and DTs.
  • Long-acting (chlordiazepoxide, diazepam) give a smoother, self-tapering course.
  • Lorazepam / oxazepam preferred in hepatic failure/cirrhosis, elderly, respiratory compromise — glucuronidation (not oxidation), no active metabolites, short half-life.
🧠 LOT — liver-safe benzos

Lorazepam · Oxazepam · Temazepam — metabolised only by glucuronidation, so they bypass a failing liver's oxidative (Phase I / CYP) machinery. Use in cirrhosis & the elderly.

Thiamine, Wernicke & Korsakoff

🔑 Thiamine BEFORE glucose

Always give thiamine before (or with) glucose — a glucose load in a thiamine-deficient patient can precipitate Wernicke encephalopathy. Give parenteral thiamine in at-risk / malnourished patients.

Wernicke encephalopathy (acute, reversible)

Triad = ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus + ataxia + confusion. Thiamine (B1) deficiency. Full triad in only ~10% — treat on suspicion. Medical emergency, reversible if caught early.

Korsakoff syndrome (chronic, irreversible)

Chronic anterograde + retrograde amnesia with confabulation, preserved consciousness. Lesion: mammillary bodies + dorsomedial thalamus.

🧠 Wernicke = "COAT"; Korsakoff = "memory + making it up"

Wernicke: Confusion · Ophthalmoplegia · Ataxia · Thiamine. Korsakoff: irreversible amnesia + confabulation (mammillary bodies).

DT management & anticonvulsants

Delirium tremens

  • IV BZD (diazepam / lorazepam) titrated to light sedation
  • Fluids; correct Mg²⁺ / K⁺ / glucose; parenteral thiamine
  • Treat the precipitant (infection, trauma)
  • Refractory → phenobarbital or dexmedetomidine / propofol (ICU adjuncts)

Anticonvulsants — role & limits

  • Carbamazepine, valproate, gabapentin: useful in mild–moderate / outpatient withdrawal
  • Do NOT prevent DTs; not first-line for severe disease
  • Phenytoin is ineffective for alcohol-withdrawal seizures
⚠ Trap

Withdrawal seizures are driven by GABA/glutamate imbalance, not a focal epileptic focus — so benzodiazepines (not phenytoin) prevent and treat them. "Phenytoin for rum fits" is a classic distractor.

5 · Neurobiology

🔑 Reward pathway

Addiction runs through the mesolimbic dopamine pathway: VTA → nucleus accumbens (with PFC + amygdala). All addictive drugs ↑ dopamine in the NAcc.

Acute alcohol

  • ↑ GABA-A → sedation, anxiolysis
  • ↓ NMDA / glutamate → cognitive slowing
  • Also ↑ endogenous opioids / β-endorphin (basis of naltrexone); affects 5-HT / 5-HT3

Chronic alcohol → withdrawal

  • Adaptive ↓ GABA-A function + ↑ NMDA/glutamate
  • On cessation → unopposed glutamatergic excitation
  • → withdrawal hyperexcitability & seizures
🧠 Kindling

Each repeated withdrawal episode lowers the seizure threshold, so every subsequent detox is more severe than the last. This is the pharmacological argument against repeated, abrupt, unmedicated detox.

CBD's multi-target ("dirty"/promiscuous) mechanism

6 · Indian Regulatory Context E

🔑 NDPS & cannabis definitions

The NDPS Act 1985 defines "cannabis (hemp)" as charas (resin/hashish), ganja (flowering/fruiting tops), and mixtures/drinks of these. Seeds and leaves (without the tops) are excluded — so bhang (a leaf preparation) sits outside the central NDPS definition and is state-regulated.

  • Pure CBD is not separately scheduled under NDPS. CBD from hemp with <0.3% THC is treated leniently; THC is controlled; marijuana-derived/high-THC CBD is prohibited.
  • Cannabis medicine is allowed under Ayurveda (Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia); CBD products marketed under AYUSH licensing. FSSAI (2021) recognized hemp seed/seed products (<0.3% THC) as food.
  • Isolated CBD is not an approved allopathic drug in India → clinical research needs CDSCO approval, ethics clearance, and CTRI registration.

⚠ Pupils — the fastest discriminator

Opioid intoxication = pinpoint (miosis); opioid withdrawal = dilated (mydriasis). Stimulant/cannabis/anticholinergic intoxication = dilated. "Pinpoint pupils + ↓RR + coma" is always the opioid-overdose stem → naloxone.

🧠 Opioid withdrawal — "the runny picture"

Everything runs and stands up: running eyes/nose (lacrimation, rhinorrhoea), goosebumps (piloerection), dilated pupils, gut cramps + diarrhoea. Miserable but not fatal (unlike alcohol/benzo withdrawal). Scale = COWS.

OST — opioid substitution, the exam favourites

Buprenorphine

  • Partial µ-agonist → ceiling effect on respiratory depression = safer
  • Sublingual; can precipitate withdrawal if given too early (high affinity displaces full agonists)
  • Often combined with naloxone (Suboxone) to deter injection

Methadone

  • Full µ-agonist, long-acting; no ceiling → OD risk higher
  • Prolongs QTc; many CYP interactions
  • Naloxone = short-acting µ-antagonist → OD may need repeat doses/infusion

4 · Mood Stabilizers & Benzodiazepines

Lithium

  • Narrow therapeutic index (0.6–1.2 mEq/L); renally excreted
  • Toxicity ↑ by NSAIDs, thiazides, ACE-i, dehydration
  • Toxicity: coarse tremor, ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, seizures
  • Chronic: hypothyroidism, nephrogenic DI, Ebstein anomaly (pregnancy)

Valproate / Carbamazepine / Lamotrigine

  • Valproate → hepatotoxic, most teratogenic (neural tube), weight gain, tremor
  • Carbamazepine → SJS (HLA-B*1502), agranulocytosis, hyponatraemia; auto-induces CYP3A4
  • Lamotrigine → best for bipolar depression; SJS if titrated fast
✓ Benzodiazepines — the addiction-psych overlap

BZDs enhance GABA-A (↑ channel-opening frequency; barbiturates ↑ duration). Cross-tolerant with alcohol → first-line in alcohol withdrawal. But BZD/barbiturate/alcohol withdrawal is potentially fatal (seizures) — unlike opioid withdrawal. OD reversal = flumazenil (caution: can precipitate seizures).

5 · Quick-Fire Psychiatry

Delirium vs Dementia

  • Delirium — acute, fluctuating, ↓attention, altered consciousness, reversible, often medical cause. Rx underlying + low-dose haloperidol
  • Dementia — chronic, progressive, clear consciousness until late, memory-led

Schizophrenia essentials

  • Dx needs sx ≥1 month (ICD) / ≥6 months (DSM-5)
  • Positive (delusions, hallucinations) vs negative (avolition, flat affect, alogia)
  • Best prognosis: acute onset, mood sx, good premorbid function
🔑 Substance-induced vs primary disorder

A psychosis/mood/anxiety syndrome that resolves after ~1 month of abstinence is substance-induced; if it persists well beyond expected intoxication/withdrawal it points to a primary (independent) disorder — a common addiction-psych stem.
