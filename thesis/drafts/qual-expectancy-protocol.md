Draft v0.1, 5 Nov 2026. Not yet reviewed by the guide. Structure: ICMR Common Forms for Ethics Committee Review (2020) headings as listed in `05` §C; confirm the current AIIMS Guwahati IEC form with the Member Secretary (open question 11). This is a **standalone study**: it can go to the IEC before the lane is chosen and before the main protocol, because it only interviews people and changes no treatment.

## 4. Methodology

### 4.1 Background (one page in the final; seeds)

- The Allen Carr method treats cultural and personal beliefs about alcohol's benefits as the core of dependence and tries to dismantle them (`03` §1). In smoking it matched or beat a specialist service in two RCTs, but **neither trial measured beliefs**, and there is **no peer-reviewed trial in alcohol** (`03` §1 table, FT).
- Alcohol outcome expectancies predict drinking, and expectancy challenge reduces expectancies and drinking in students, but the effect fades by 3–6 months and the evidence is almost entirely from Western college samples (Scott-Sheldon 2012; Wang X 2026; `03` §2 row 3).
- Expectancy questionnaires were built in Western cultures. They may miss beliefs that matter in Assam: locally brewed rice beer and its ritual and social roles, beliefs that alcohol is needed for hard physical work, sleep, pain or cold, family beliefs about a husband's or father's drinking, and beliefs about medicines and about quitting. Nobody has described these beliefs on an Indian detox ward.
- An intervention that targets beliefs must first know which beliefs to target, in the patients' own words.

### 4.2 Rationale and gap

No qualitative study of alcohol-related beliefs among inpatients with alcohol dependence in Northeast India has been located (search to be documented in the final protocol). Families are central to treatment in India but their beliefs are rarely studied alongside patients'. Staff beliefs shape what patients hear on the ward.

### 4.3 Research questions

1. What do inpatients with alcohol dependence believe alcohol gives them, and how did they come to believe it?
2. What do they believe stopping drinking will cost them, and what do they fear about it?
3. What do their family members believe alcohol does for the patient, and about stopping?
4. What beliefs do ward staff hold and convey about alcohol, craving, willpower and recovery?
5. How acceptable would patients, families and staff find a programme that questions these beliefs, and in what format, language and setting?

### 4.4 Objectives

1. Describe and categorise beliefs about the benefits of alcohol and the costs of stopping, by group.
2. Produce a **local expectancy catalogue**: a list of beliefs, each with example wording in Assamese, Hindi, Bengali or English, its frequency across interviews, and whether existing expectancy scales (AEQ, CEOA) already cover it.
3. Map findings to the 16-row construct map in `03` §2 and note beliefs the map does not cover.
4. Describe acceptability requirements for a belief-reversal programme (format, group or individual, family role, video, WhatsApp, language, who delivers it).

### 4.5 Design and approach

Qualitative descriptive design with semi-structured, one-to-one, in-person interviews. Analysis by the framework method (Gale et al. 2013, *BMC Med Res Methodol*), combining a deductive coding frame from the `03` §2 construct map with inductive codes. Reporting by COREQ.

### 4.6 Setting and timing

Psychiatry inpatient ward, AIIMS Guwahati. Patients are interviewed **after medically managed withdrawal has settled and before discharge** (typically days 5–12 of admission), when CIWA-Ar is below 10 and the treating team confirms capacity (section 5.3). Family members are interviewed on the ward or in a private room during visiting hours, separately from the patient. Staff are interviewed at a time agreed with their supervisor, in a private room.

### 4.7 Sample and sampling

| Group | Target n | Purposive sampling dimensions |
|---|---|---|
| Inpatients with alcohol dependence | 20–25 | Age bands; sex (seek women deliberately, though few are admitted); preferred language (Assamese, Hindi, Bengali, other); rural/urban; tea-garden community; tribal community where local rice beer is customary; first admission vs repeat; severity (SADQ); main beverage (country liquor, rice beer, IMFL, mixed) |
| Family members | 10 | Spouse, parent, adult child; living with the patient; sex |
| Ward staff | 8 | Nurses, resident doctors, clinical psychologist, psychiatric social worker; years of experience |

Sample size is guided by **information power** (Malterud et al. 2016, *Qual Health Res*) rather than a fixed saturation number. The study aim is fairly narrow, the sample is specific, an established framework guides analysis, and interviews are in depth, so 20–25 patients should give adequate information power. Recruitment stops for a group when two consecutive interviews add no new belief category to the catalogue (stopping rule recorded in the audit trail), but not before 15 patients, 8 family members and 6 staff are interviewed.

### 4.8 Data collection

- **Interviewer.** A trained interviewer who is **not part of the participant's treating team** for that admission, to reduce pressure to give the "right" answers. If the PI is on the treating rota, the PI does not interview patients they are treating; a co-investigator or trained research assistant does. This matters because the PI is a treatment provider on the ward (declared in the conflict-of-interest form, `05` §B).
- **Language.** In the participant's preferred language; interviewers fluent in that language; a bilingual research assistant joins if needed.
- **Length.** 30–60 minutes; patients may split into two sittings if tired.
- **Recording.** Audio-recorded with separate consent; field notes after each interview; no video.
- **Short sample description form** (not an outcome): age, sex, education, occupation, language, rural/urban, community, main beverage, years of drinking, previous quit attempts, previous admissions, SADQ and AUDIT scores taken from the clinical record with consent (no new scales are needed for this study, so it does not wait for the scale translation work).

### 4.9 Topic guides (summary; full guides in Annex B)

**Patients.**
1. Story: first drink, how drinking changed over the years, what led to this admission.
2. What alcohol gives you now: probe each domain only if the participant has not raised it (relaxation, sleep, pain, work stamina, courage, confidence, social life, festivals and rituals, taste, forgetting problems, sex, appetite). For each: how do you know it does that? Was it true before you started drinking? Do non-drinkers have it?
3. Where the beliefs came from: family, friends, workplace, films, songs, advertisements, community customs, rice beer at home.
4. What stopping would take away: losses, fears (of failure, of a life that is boring, of losing friends, of withdrawal), what has happened at past attempts, and what you think made them fail.
5. Willpower and craving: what a craving feels like, what you tell yourself, what you think a craving means.
6. Medicines: what you believe about anti-craving medicines and about "tablets for alcohol".
7. Identity: how you see yourself (drinker, patient, addict, someone who drinks too much), how you would like to see yourself.
8. Acceptability: if a programme asked you to look hard at each of these beliefs, would you join? Group or alone? With family? Videos, phone messages, WhatsApp? Who should run it: a doctor, a counsellor, someone who used to drink?

**Family members.** What drinking does for the patient, in your view; what you believe about why he or she drinks; what you fear about stopping; what helped or hurt in past attempts; your role; what support you would want; acceptability as above.

**Staff.** What patients say alcohol gives them; what staff tell patients about craving, willpower and recovery; beliefs about who recovers; views on a belief-focused programme; practical constraints on the ward (time, space, staffing, languages).

Guides are piloted with 2 patients and 1 family member; pilot interviews are kept in the analysis if the guide changes only slightly.

### 4.10 Analysis

1. Verbatim transcription in the source language; translation into English by a bilingual translator; 10% of transcripts back-checked by a second bilingual against the audio. Culturally specific terms are kept in the original language with a gloss.
2. Framework method: familiarisation; initial coding of 4–5 transcripts by two coders independently; agreement on a working analytic framework (deductive codes from the `03` §2 rows plus inductive codes); indexing of all transcripts; charting into a matrix (participants × categories) by group; mapping and interpretation.
3. **Double coding of 20% of transcripts**, discrepancies discussed and resolved; no kappa (it is not meaningful for this design), but an audit trail of codebook changes.
4. Analysis runs alongside data collection so later interviews can probe new beliefs.
5. Outputs: belief catalogue (objective 2), comparison across groups (patients vs families vs staff), map against the AEQ/CEOA item content (which beliefs the scales miss), acceptability requirements table.
6. Software: Taguette or QualCoder (free) or NVivo.

### 4.11 Rigour and reflexivity

COREQ checklist. Reflexive notes kept by every interviewer. The PI's position as a psychiatrist on the same ward is stated in the methods. Any further reflexivity statement follows the disclosure decision recorded in `03` §9; the protocol wording does not depend on that decision. Member checking: a short summary of findings is discussed with 3–5 willing participants (patients in follow-up clinic, family members) before the paper is finalised.

## 5. Recruitment and participants

### 5.1 Inclusion

- **Patients:** age ≥ 18; ICD-11 alcohol dependence (DSM-5-TR moderate–severe AUD) confirmed by the treating psychiatrist; admitted for detoxification; withdrawal settled (CIWA-Ar < 10) and no delirium; able to converse in Assamese, Hindi, Bengali or English; gives informed consent.
- **Family members:** age ≥ 18; lives with or is the main carer of an inpatient with alcohol dependence (the patient need not be a study participant, but if they are, both consent separately); able to converse in one of the study languages.
- **Staff:** currently working on the psychiatry inpatient service for ≥ 3 months; gives informed consent.

### 5.2 Exclusion

Patients: delirium or Wernicke–Korsakoff features this admission; active psychosis or mania; current suicidal ideation with intent (managed clinically, not interviewed); cognitive impairment that prevents meaningful consent on clinical assessment (MoCA used as a screen; cut-off to be agreed with the guide, accounting for education); too unwell in the treating team's judgement.

### 5.3 Capacity and vulnerability

Inpatients with alcohol dependence are a vulnerable group: dependent on the treating team, possibly cognitively affected in early abstinence. Safeguards: consent only after withdrawal has settled; capacity confirmed by a clinician not seeking consent; a teach-back check of the PIS (purpose, voluntary, can stop, no effect on care); consent taken by someone who is not the participant's treating doctor; a 24-hour gap between information and consent where the stay allows. Refusal or withdrawal has no effect on care, and the treating team is not told who declined.

### 5.4 Relationship to the ICMR cannabidiol trial

Refer to the trial by its public title only. This study changes no treatment and collects no trial data. Whether inpatients enrolled in the CBD trial may also take part is **for the trial PI to decide** (open question 6); until the answer is in writing, exclude CBD-trial participants. Do not use any trial CRF, SOP or list to identify participants.

## 6. Benefits and risks

- **Benefits:** no direct benefit; some participants may find it useful to talk about their drinking. Indirect benefit: a better-fitting programme for future patients.
- **Risks:** distress when recalling losses or family conflict; **cue-induced craving** when describing drinking in detail; worry about confidentiality, especially for family members talking about the patient and staff talking about the service.
- **Mitigation:** a distress protocol (Annex D): pause, offer to stop, check craving on a 0–10 scale before leaving if the participant raised it, inform the treating team only with the participant's agreement **unless there is a risk to safety**; the interviewer is trained in this protocol; interviews end with a neutral close (plans for the rest of the day). Family and patient interviews are never shared with each other. Staff quotations are attributed only by broad role and are checked for identifiability before publication.

## 7. Informed consent

- Separate PIS and ICF for **three groups (patients, family members, staff) in three languages (English, Hindi, Assamese)** = 9 documents, dialogue style, no discrepancy between versions (`05` §B). Bengali versions if the IEC permits and Barak Valley patients are expected.
- Separate tick-box consent for audio-recording and for anonymised quotations.
- Witness for participants who cannot read; thumb impression with witness signature.
- Audio-visual recording of consent: not required for a non-regulatory behavioural study; confirm with the IEC.
- A ~300-word lay summary for the IEC.

## 8. Payment and compensation

Reimbursement of travel and a meal for family members; refreshment for patients. Amounts to be fixed with the IEC so they are not an inducement. Compensation for research-related injury: minimal-risk study; state this and follow the IEC's instructions.

## 9. Storage and confidentiality

- Audio files moved within 24 hours to an encrypted, password-protected institute drive; deleted from the recorder at once; **audio destroyed after transcripts are verified** (or kept for the period the IEC requires, then destroyed).
- Transcripts pseudonymised at transcription (names, villages, workplaces removed); the key linking codes to identities kept separately by the PI.
- No identifiable data on personal phones, messaging apps or cloud services outside the institute. **DPDP Act 2023** statement: purpose limitation, consent, retention period, the participant's right to withdraw their data until analysis is complete (date stated in the PIS).
- Retention: per IEC/ICMR guidance (commonly 3–5 years after publication; confirm).

## 10. Publication, benefit sharing, IPR

Paper 2 of Lane 2 (`03` §11; target *Transcult Psychiatry*, *Asian J Psychiatry* or *SSM Mental Health*). Anonymised quotations only. The expectancy catalogue feeds the BRI-AD manual, which is institute/candidate IP per institute policy. Findings shared with the department and, in lay form, displayed for patients and families on the ward.

### Annex A (draft): Patient information sheet, English, dialogue style

> **What is this study?** We want to understand what people who are being treated for alcohol problems believe alcohol does for them, and what they think stopping would mean. We are also talking to family members and staff.
>
> **Why are you being asked?** Because you are being treated on this ward for alcohol problems and your withdrawal has settled.
>
> **What will you be asked to do?** Talk with a researcher for about 30 to 60 minutes, in the language you prefer. We will ask about your drinking, what alcohol gives you, what you think about stopping, and what kind of help would suit you. We would like to audio-record the talk.
>
> **Do you have to take part?** No. Saying no will not change your treatment in any way. Your doctors will not be told whether you said yes or no. You can stop the talk at any time, skip any question, and withdraw what you said until [date].
>
> **Could it upset you?** Talking about drinking can bring up difficult feelings or a craving. The researcher will check on you, and you can take a break or stop. If you want, the researcher can tell your treating team.
>
> **Who will know what you said?** Only the research team. We will remove your name and anything that could identify you. Your family will not be told what you said, and you will not be told what they said. The recording will be destroyed after it has been typed and checked.
>
> **Will you get anything?** There is no payment. [Refreshment.] What we learn will help design better treatment for people like you.
>
> **Who to contact:** [PI name, phone]; [IEC Member Secretary, phone].

Hindi and Assamese versions: to be produced by forward translation, back-translation and lay review (same process as `scale-translation-plan.md` §3, shortened), then checked by the IEC for consistency.
