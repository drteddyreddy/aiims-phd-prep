# 03 — Lane 2: A belief-reversal ("counter-brainwashing") intervention for alcohol dependence

Working academic name: **Belief-Reversal Intervention for Alcohol Dependence (BRI-AD)**. Patient-facing name to be chosen with patients (ideas in section 6). Do not use "Easyway" or "Allen Carr" in the intervention's name; the brand is trademarked and the training is proprietary. Cite the books as the inspiration and the method's logic as prior art.

---

## 1. What the Allen Carr method actually does

Reconstructed from the books (*The Easy Way to Stop Smoking*; *The Easy Way to Control Alcohol* / *Quit Drinking Without Willpower*) and the seminar descriptions. The method is a single, dense persuasion event (a book read over days, or a 5–7 hour group seminar led by a former user, with booster sessions) that aims to **remove the desire** to use rather than to build resistance against it.

### Core propositions, in Carr's own logic
1. **The trap.** The drug creates a low-grade discomfort (withdrawal, "the little monster"). The next dose relieves that discomfort. The relief is misread as pleasure or benefit. Non-users feel this way all the time; the user is only chasing the non-user's baseline.
2. **The brainwashing ("the big monster").** Society, advertising, family modelling, films, and the user's own rationalisations install the beliefs that the substance gives pleasure, relaxation, confidence, courage, concentration, social ease, stress relief, and that life without it is deprived and quitting is agony. These beliefs, not the chemical, are the real addiction.
3. **Therefore there is nothing to give up.** Every perceived benefit is examined and shown to be either an illusion or a relief of a problem the drug itself caused.
4. **Fear is the jailer.** Fear of withdrawal, fear of failure, fear of life without the crutch. The method argues these fears are products of the drug and dissolve once the beliefs do.
5. **Willpower methods fail by design.** They preserve the belief that a sacrifice is being made, which produces deprivation, which produces relapse. If desire is gone, no willpower is needed.
6. **Specific illusions dismantled one by one.** Taste, boredom, concentration, relaxation, stress, social confidence, celebration, "I have an addictive personality", "just one won't hurt", "I'll be miserable".
7. **Procedural instructions.** Keep using while you read (so belief change happens without anticipatory deprivation); do not cut down (scarcity raises perceived value); no substitutes; do not envy users, pity them; make a final, certain decision; perform a final-use ritual; never doubt the decision afterwards; treat residual withdrawal as the monster dying, with curiosity rather than dread; adopt the identity "I am a non-drinker" at once, not "an alcoholic who is trying".
8. **Delivery by a former user**, which carries credibility and models the outcome.

### What the evidence says
| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Keogan, Li & Clancy 2019, *Tobacco Control* (Ireland, n=300, ACE vs Quit.ie) | Higher quit rates at 1, 3, 6, 12 months in the Allen Carr arm; weight gain 3.8 kg vs 1.8 kg. Single centre. |
| Frings et al. 2020, *Addiction* (London, n=620, ACE vs NHS specialist service with NRT) | 26-week abstinence 19.4% vs 14.8%; CI includes 1; authors: no clear evidence of a difference, i.e., at least comparable to gold standard. Funded by Allen Carr's Easyway Ltd. Participants were "invited to restructure their personal beliefs about smoking". |
| Systematic review 2023, *Tobacco Prevention & Cessation* (doi 10.18332/tpc/172314) | Collates the above; component-level evidence absent (**verify** full text). |
| Book-only RCT in head-and-neck cancer patients | Reading the book alone did not improve cessation. |
| NICE NG209 surveillance 2020 | Noted both RCTs; the method is not currently a recommended NHS intervention. |
| **Alcohol** | **No peer-reviewed trial exists.** Company success figures are refund-based, not outcome-based. |

Reading: the smoking data say a one-day belief-reversal event is at least as effective as specialist pharmacological-behavioural support. Nobody has (a) tested it in alcohol, (b) tested it in a dependent clinical population, (c) measured the proposed mechanism, (d) dismantled its components, (e) adapted it to a non-Western culture or to low literacy, or (f) delivered it after medical detox where the "keep using while you read" instruction is impossible and unsafe. All six are open.

## 2. Mapping Carr's propositions onto addiction science

This mapping is what turns a self-help method into a thesis-grade intervention. Each row gives you a construct to measure.

| Carr proposition | Scientific construct | Key literature | Measurable by |
|---|---|---|---|
| The little monster; relief misread as pleasure | Negative reinforcement; opponent-process; allostasis | Solomon & Corbit; Koob & Le Moal | Withdrawal-relief drinking motives (DMQ-R coping subscale) |
| Pleasure is an illusion; you only want it | **Incentive sensitisation: "wanting" dissociates from "liking"** | Robinson & Berridge 1993–2016 | Cue-reactivity VAS wanting vs liking; implicit measures |
| The big monster (brainwashing) | **Alcohol outcome expectancies**; expectancy challenge reduces drinking | Goldman; Darkes & Goldman 1993; Wiers 2005; meta-analysis g = −0.20 at 1 month | AEQ, CEOA (positive expectancy subscales) |
| Fear keeps you using | Anxiety sensitivity; experiential avoidance | ACT literature | AAQ-II; Fear of abstinence items (custom) |
| Willpower fails; deprivation causes relapse | Abstinence violation effect; restraint theory | Marlatt & Gordon | Decisional balance; AASE temptation vs confidence |
| Nothing to give up; decision made with certainty | Cognitive dissonance; counter-attitudinal advocacy; ambivalence resolution | Festinger; Miller & Rollnick; 2025 mediator study of counter-attitudinal advocacy | Dissonance thermometer; URICA; readiness rulers |
| "I am a non-drinker now" | **Identity change** (PRIME theory); self-concept | West 2006; Tombor et al. | Drinker identity scale; single-item identity rulers |
| Keep using while you read, then one decision | Belief updating under prediction error; memory destabilisation and rewriting | Das & Kamboj (your ketamine background); Hon et al. 2015 | Not directly; design feature |
| Residual craving as the monster dying | Reappraisal; metacognitive beliefs about craving; desire thinking | Caselli & Spada | Desire Thinking Questionnaire; Metacognitions about craving |
| Delivered by a former user | Peer credibility; recorded recovery narratives; connection mechanisms | NEON programme (Slade, Rennick-Egglestone); KLIFAD | Narrative connection measures; engagement |

Logic in one sentence: **BRI-AD is a structured, emotionally charged expectancy challenge plus identity reframe, delivered with peer credibility, timed to the post-detox window, whose hypothesised proximal effects are reduced positive expectancies and desire thinking and increased abstinence self-efficacy, which in turn reduce craving and relapse.**

## 3. Necessary departures from Carr for a dependent, post-detox, Indian population

These are not softenings; they are safety and evidence requirements. State them in the protocol as design decisions.

1. **No "keep drinking while you learn."** Unsupervised cessation in alcohol dependence causes seizures and delirium. Your participants are already detoxed inpatients; the belief work happens on days 5–12 of admission, after CIWA-Ar is low.
2. **No opposition to medication.** Carr's anti-substitute stance is right for nicotine gum and wrong for naltrexone, acamprosate, baclofen, disulfiram. The module reframes medication as "a seatbelt, not a crutch" and keeps it in treatment as usual. Anything else would be unethical and would get the protocol rejected.
3. **No promise of "easy."** The thesis claim is "removes the sense of sacrifice", not "removes all discomfort".
4. **Family in the room.** Indian alcohol dependence is managed with families; the brainwashing audit should include family-held beliefs ("he needs it to sleep", "a man drinks").
5. **Low-literacy delivery.** Video, illustrated workbook, oral group discussion, local language. The book is replaced by facilitated sessions.
6. **Screen for comorbidity.** Psychosis, bipolar, suicidality, cognitive impairment (MoCA) exclude or defer.
7. **Group dynamics.** Three to six patients, mixed severity, facilitator trained to handle contempt-for-users content without shaming current drinkers.

## 4. Logic model

```
INPUTS                 ACTIVITIES (5 ward sessions + aftercare)        PROXIMAL MECHANISMS (2–4 wk)        OUTCOMES (12–24 wk)
Manual v1, videos      S1 The Trap (how wanting is built)              ↓ positive alcohol expectancies      ↓ craving (PACS, OCDS)
Facilitator (ex-user   S2 Dismantling my benefits (expectancy audit)   ↓ desire thinking                    ↓ relapse to heavy drinking
 or trained PSW)       S3 Fear and willpower (why past quits failed)   ↑ abstinence self-efficacy           ↑ % days abstinent
Workbook, family       S4 Identity and the decision (ceremony)         ↑ non-drinker identity               ↑ engagement with aftercare
Smartphone/WhatsApp    S5 Living free ("just one", cues, celebration)  ↓ fear of abstinence                 ↑ QoL, family burden ↓
                       Aftercare: 6 booster videos, recovery
                       narratives, 90-second "remember the trap"
                       craving tool, weekly check-in
```

Moderators to prespecify: severity (SADQ), baseline expectancies, literacy, family involvement, medication at discharge.

## 5. Module architecture (v1 for the manual)

Each ward session 45–60 min, group of 3–6, days 5–12 of admission, facilitator + co-facilitator, workbook pages filled in session, 5–7 min video opens each session.

| Session | Title | Content | Carr element | Technique | Homework |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The trap | Personal drinking timeline; how the first drinks felt vs now; the relief loop explained with a drawn graph of mood across a drinking day; "wanting vs liking" in plain words | Little monster, big monster | Psychoeducation, Socratic questioning | List every benefit alcohol gives you |
| 2 | Auditing the benefits | Each listed benefit tested: Did it exist before you drank? Does a non-drinker lack it? Does it return when you stop? Family adds its beliefs. Each is classed illusion / drug-caused problem / real but replaceable | Dismantling illusions | Expectancy challenge; counter-attitudinal advocacy (patient argues the case to the group) | Notice a craving and write what it promised |
| 3 | Fear and willpower | Review of past quit attempts; identify the deprivation story; abstinence violation effect; fear inventory; reframing withdrawal pangs as the monster starving | Fear; willpower trap | Reappraisal; decisional balance with the "benefits" column now empty | Tell one family member the three illusions you found |
| 4 | The decision | Identity work: drinker → non-drinker; the certainty exercise; written "goodbye letter" to alcohol read aloud; family witness; celebration (replaces the final-drink ritual, which is impossible on a ward) | Final decision ritual; identity | Commitment, public declaration, dissonance consolidation | Record a 60-second phone video to your future self |
| 5 | Living free | Cues, social pressure, festivals, "just one" trap, how to answer offers, aftercare plan, medication as seatbelt, what to do in a craving (the 90-second tool), relapse response without self-contempt | Just one; pity not envy | Marlatt relapse prevention reframed | Install/receive aftercare |
| Aftercare (wk 1–12) | Boosters | Six 4–6 min videos (one per week for 6 weeks), 3–5 recorded recovery narratives (Assamese/Hindi/Bengali, with one anonymised narrative drawn from your own experience if you choose), 90-second craving tool on demand, weekly 2-question check-in with facilitator reply | Booster seminars; ex-user credibility | Narrative connection; spaced retrieval | — |

Delivery platform for v1: **WhatsApp broadcast + voice-note replies.** Reasons: ASER 2024 reports > 90% of rural Assam households own a smartphone; a custom app adds 6–9 months and DPDP obligations; WhatsApp allows video, voice, and low literacy. Move to an app (or ride the department's ANRF continuity-of-care platform) in v2 once content is validated.

Fidelity: session checklist (10 items), 20% of sessions audio-recorded and rated by a second rater, facilitator manual with scripts.

## 6. Naming and framing for patients

Options to test with patients: *Mukti Path* (path to freedom), *Phanda* ("the trap") series, *Nesha-mukt mon* (Assamese: mind free of intoxication), *Bina Zor* (without force / willpower-free). Avoid medicalised names; the method's persuasive power partly comes from not sounding like treatment.

## 7. Development and evaluation pathway

Framework: MRC/NIHR 2021 complex-intervention framework (develop → feasibility → evaluation → implementation; core elements: programme theory, stakeholder engagement, context, uncertainties, refinement) combined with ORBIT phases (Ia define, Ib refine, IIa proof-of-concept, IIb feasibility pilot, III efficacy). Report the intervention with TIDieR; report the pilot with CONSORT-extension for pilot trials.

| Phase | Months | Work | Output / paper |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1a Define** | 1–6 | Systematic/scoping review of belief-targeting and Allen Carr-type interventions across substances; qualitative study: 20–25 detox inpatients + 10 family members + 8 staff on "what alcohol gives you / what stopping takes away" (framework analysis) to build the local expectancy catalogue; programme theory and logic model; manual v1 | Paper 1 (review), Paper 2 (qualitative expectancy study) |
| **1b Refine** | 6–10 | Co-design: 3 workshops with 6–8 people in recovery (experts by experience) and facilitators; expert panel content validity (5–7 addiction psychiatrists/psychologists rate each component); translation and back-translation (Assamese, Hindi, Bengali); video production; facilitator training | Manual v2, TIDieR description |
| **2a Proof of concept** | 10–16 | Single-arm, n = 30 consecutive eligible inpatients; feasibility (eligibility, consent, retention, session attendance, aftercare engagement), acceptability (CSQ-8, exit interviews), fidelity; pre-post change in expectancies, desire thinking, self-efficacy, identity, PACS at 4 weeks; 12-week drinking; refine | Paper 3 (development + proof-of-concept, e.g., *JMIR Formative Research* or *Pilot and Feasibility Studies*) |
| **2b Pilot / feasibility RCT** | 16–36 | BRI-AD + TAU vs attention-matched psychoeducation + TAU (preferred) or TAU alone; 1:1; assessor-blind; primary feasibility + craving at 4 weeks; secondary relapse at 12 and 24 weeks; mediation (expectancy change → craving → drinking) | Paper 4 (pilot RCT), protocol paper optional |
| Writing | 36–44 | Thesis | — |

Why an attention-matched control: a belief-reversal intervention versus nothing will "work" through attention and expectation. Psychoeducation of equal dose is the control that lets you claim a specific mechanism. Discuss with your guide; TAU-only is acceptable for a feasibility pilot if resources are tight.

| Fear / avoidance | AAQ-II; custom fear-of-abstinence items | Exploratory |
| Readiness | URICA or SOCRATES | Baseline covariate |
| Severity | SADQ, AUDIT | Stratifier |
| Mood | PHQ-9, GAD-7 | Covariate |
| Acceptability | CSQ-8; exit interviews | Feasibility |
| Engagement | Session attendance; video views; check-in replies | Feasibility |
| Family | Family burden scale (optional) | Secondary |

### Sample sizes (80% power, two-sided α 0.05, +20% attrition)
| Outcome | Assumed effect | Per arm | With attrition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanism (expectancies) | d = 0.8 (expectancy challenges produce large proximal belief change) | 25 | 32 |
| Craving (PACS) | d = 0.5 | 63 | 79 |
| Craving, optimistic | d = 0.6 | 44 | 55 |
| Relapse at 12 wk | 60% vs 35% | 62 | 78 |

Interpretation: a pilot RCT of **N = 64 (32/arm)** is powered on the mechanism and gives feasibility estimates; **N = 110–160** is a definitive trial. Recommend the thesis commits to the pilot (N = 64–80) with the definitive trial as the post-PhD grant.

## 9. Your own story: options and ethics

The intervention is "inspired by" eight years of your own dependence and recovery through this logic. There are three ways to handle that.

| Option | What it means | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Anonymous authorship | The manual and narratives are "developed with input from people with lived experience"; your story becomes one anonymised composite narrative among 3–5 recorded by consenting people in recovery | Protects you; standard practice; no change in scientific value | Loses the "ex-user in the room" credibility element unless a co-facilitator in recovery is recruited |
| B. Disclosure within the team only | Guide and co-facilitators know; the protocol notes "a team member with lived experience contributed to content"; you do not disclose to participants | Allows reflexivity statements in qualitative papers (good practice); keeps patient boundary | Still a small circle; decide who |
| C. Public disclosure | You are named as a person in recovery in the thesis and papers | Destigmatising; strongest authenticity; aligns with the "researchers with addiction" commentary (2022) | Irreversible; you are a central-government contractual employee with a stated plan for European applications; dual-role and boundary issues on your own ward; needs supervision |

Recommendation: **A or B for the thesis.** Build the peer-credibility element by recruiting a co-facilitator in stable recovery (≥ 2 years) from the ward's alumni or a local self-help group, trained and paid. Decide C, if ever, as a separate personal decision after the PhD. Whatever you choose, the protocol should include a reflexivity statement and a plan for your own supervision and wellbeing, since you will be running sessions that revisit your own history.

Related ethics: lived-experience co-designers must be paid, credited, and given a safety plan; some IECs restrict cash payments, so budget vouchers or honoraria through the institute.
